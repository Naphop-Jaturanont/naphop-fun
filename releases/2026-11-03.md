## Learning and interaction
Predict the earliest reply, send a hello to the Moon or Mars, and distinguish delivery from acknowledgement. Compare near/far Mars, experiment with a one-way-delay slider, use real-time or visibly marked 120× playback, and jump to delivery/reply boundaries. Three discovery cards reveal why even an ideal connection cannot erase light-travel time. No orbital or rover simulation is claimed.

## Verified sources and assumptions
Read on Nov 3, 2026:
- NIST, https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/meet-constants : c = 299,792,458 m/s; exact SI value converted to 299,792.458 km/s.
- NASA, https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/moon-distance/en/ : mean Earth–Moon distance 384,400 km, not current separation.
- NASA/JPL, https://science.nasa.gov/blog/speaking-percys-language-how-to-talk-so-perseverance-will-listen/ : radio communication to Mars takes approximately 3–22 minutes one way depending on orbital positions.

Moon model: distance / c = approximately 1.28222 s one way, 2.56444 s round trip. Mars uses example propagation delays in NASA's approximate range; displayed distance is derived from the selected delay, not live orbital data or exact orbital extrema. Destinations are fixed and answer immediately. Processing, transmission duration, queues, relays and solar interference omitted. Therefore reply values are ideal lower bounds for this model, not promised real-world latency. Artwork is explicitly not to scale. Fast-forward/jump affect playback, not physical signal speed.

## Design and originality
Studied NASA's public explanation of communication timing and NASA Space Place's prediction/comparison activity. Hypothesis: separating the outbound and return events exposes the common misconception that delivery time equals reply time. Actual local interaction verified each boundary and feedback. This is an original visual and implementation, with no Neal.fun or third-party code/assets copied. No new packages installed.
